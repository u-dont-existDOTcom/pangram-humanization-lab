Building Enough Safety to Stay Present

Somatic Experiencing

If even getting near the material sends me over the edge, I don't need the whole story first. I can notice a sensation, orient to the room, catch an impulse or defensive response, and slow down. That's already enough to work with.

Titration and pendulation are basically going in a little and coming back. I touch as much activation as I can honestly tolerate, see what happens, then return toward something steadier. Some people spend three to six months learning to do that, and severe developmental trauma can take longer. Those numbers are a rough orientation, not a schedule. If somebody is already stable and has one clear target, I wouldn't make them wait arbitrary months before adding focused trauma work.

Trauma-Sensitive / Restorative Yoga

By yoga, I mean trauma-sensitive yoga, restorative yoga, gentle somatic yoga, or very gentle yin with props, short holds, and an easy exit—not power yoga, hot yoga, or somebody announcing, “Open your hips and release your trauma.”

A teacher can keep the choice visible: “You might try…” “You can keep your eyes open.” “Come out of the shape whenever you want.” “Is this useful, neutral, or too much?” “There can be several right versions. Pick the one that fits your body.”

I would leave out intense stretching, breath retention, forced breathwork, aggressive hip opening, long passive holds, hands-on adjustments without clear consent, and any instruction to push through because suffering is supposed to be therapeutic. Once I can't choose, I'm done.

Gentle Shaking / TRE

Shaking is the same: small means small. Basic TRE, very light neurogenic tremoring, gentle shaking while standing, or a short bit of playful movement may be enough. I am not trying to blast trauma out of my body. If it becomes compulsive, frightening, dissociative, or hard to stop, it has gone too far.

If the tremor reaches your neck while you are lying down, do not let your unsupported head whip back and forth. Stop or make the movement smaller; support your head, or have an experienced provider help support it, and get guidance before trying again. Forceful, rapid back-and-forth neck movement can injure the neck.
