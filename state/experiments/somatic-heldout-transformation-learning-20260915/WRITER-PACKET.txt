=== TRAINING PAIR 1 ===
AI-SHAPED BEFORE:
Somatic Experiencing makes sense to me as an early option when the main problem is that activation runs away with you. Instead of forcing a detailed trauma narrative, it pays attention to sensation, orientation, impulse, defensive response, and pacing. The basic skill is noticing activation without immediately drowning in it.

This is where the usual language about expanding your “window of tolerance” becomes practical. Titration, pendulation, and orientation are all ways of asking: can you touch something difficult in a small enough dose, notice what happens, and come back?

MANUAL HUMAN AFTER:
I put Somatic Experiencing up at the beginning because it helps people to reconnect with their body, to begin to feel it again. And as you can see from my intro, it’s founder, Peter Levine, has developed what I consider the most comprehensive framework for understanding trauma and the way the body releases it.

Imagine your body is like your child (I keep falling into that analogy 😛). Your child is saying “Mommy, daddy, I need something.” But you’re busy with your work so you don’t hear. After a while, your baby boo is going nuts because you don’t listen, and s/he starts screaming and throwing a fit. Now you yourself are “triggered” or “flooded” and screaming, and you don’t even know why. But if you could just learn to listen to your body before it becomes a crisis, that could solve a lot of problems before they even start!

The usual therapy theory here talks about expanding your “window of tolerance,” but I think my analogy is a better representation of what Levine is trying to say. He uses the concepts of titration (going from the shallow end of the pool slowly deeper), pendulation (going in and out of the deep end to learn safety there), and orientation (coming out of the pool, drying off, noticing you’re safe). That’s the beautiful thing about not actually being in the trauma. Since it’s a memory, you can approach it in an optimal and safe way, bit by bit.

=== TRAINING PAIR 2 ===
AI-SHAPED BEFORE:
CBT can show up much earlier than this. Stage 5 is where I mean the bigger narrative stuff: revisiting old beliefs, building a coherent life story, separating past danger from present reality, reclaiming agency, and figuring out what I actually want my life to be now—my values, boundaries, relationships, identity, direction, and sometimes forgiveness if that is relevant. Inner-child reparenting can deepen here too.

MANUAL HUMAN AFTER:
Once we’ve tuned into our body, our subconscious, and moved the energy in the ways it needed to move, there’s still the cognitive integration layer, because we’re actually humans, not just animals. This is the part that insurance often does pay for to start with (Cognitive Behavioral Therapy, etc.), although this guide is predicated on the idea that it’s not always the best place to start.

When I say cognitive integration, I’m talking about revisiting old beliefs, identities, and stories, now that their foundations might have crumbled a bit. We need to rebuild all that so it makes sense now that we’re seeing clearer. When we re-evaluate and start to see that maybe we are actually safe now, we can also take back our power. And at the same time, we might even realize we need to apologize to some folks for painting a picture that we wouldn’t paint about them anymore. We may begin to forgive, and ask for forgiveness. Conversely, we might realize we need to put up better boundaries with people that we let in too easily.

Inner-child reparenting is another form of integration therapy, although it’s more all-encompassing and less purely cognitive, so it can fit in this layer as well as beyond it.

=== TRAINING PAIR 3 ===
AI-SHAPED BEFORE:
The amount of published research behind a modality matters, but it is not a personal treatment oracle. A thin literature does not prove a method ineffective; a large literature does not prove it is the right first method for you. Start from first principles, look at what seems to help people with similar patterns, try one change at a time, and judge it by what happens in your actual life.

MANUAL HUMAN AFTER:
It is good to do some research before jumping into a long course of therapy, especially if it will cost a lot in time or money. But even if you have a zillion studies saying this therapy has a large effect size on the thing you’re treating, it’s still interesting to talk to people who’ve done these therapies and see how they were before vs after. It’s still important to see if they make some kind of sense, and finally, you still have to dip your feet in and see if it’s a fit for you.

Brainspotting is one option I would look at for this kind of material. The method uses gaze position, focused attention, bodily awareness, and therapist attunement to stay with emotionally charged material that may be difficult to narrate.

Brainspotting can also prepare for inner-child reparenting or run alongside it, depending on how it is used. A resource-oriented or carefully titrated session is different from using Brainspotting to enter highly charged developmental material.

Brainspotting interests me especially for diffuse developmental trauma, pre-verbal or body-held material, trauma that has not responded well to purely cognitive approaches, and those emotional knots where the body seems to know more than the story. You do not necessarily need to explain every detail for the session to move.
