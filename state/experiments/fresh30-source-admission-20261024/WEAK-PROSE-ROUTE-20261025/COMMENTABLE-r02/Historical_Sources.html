Grief and receiving care

Some people find that deep grieving gives way to warmth, connection, or a bodily sense of being held by the universe. That is a description of an experience, and it is not evidence for any particular physiological mechanism. Love can also open grief from the other direction: being cared for may bring sadness about the care that was missing, which does not mean the love failed. Stay with the experience when you can remain present and keep your choice about it. More tears, by themselves, do not prove more healing.

Borrowing a caring adult

You can borrow care without a history full of trustworthy people. If it helps, use a remembered person, or imagine an adult who intends to take good care of you. Ask what that person would say or do given your actual history, limitations, and options. If warmth feels out of reach, start with the least cruel response you can believe. Once you find one usable sentence or action, try it instead of repeating the exercise in different words.

Pacing trauma work

This is where the familiar talk of widening your "window of tolerance" becomes practical. Titration, pendulation, and orientation each ask the same question: can you touch something difficult in a small enough dose, notice what happens, and return? Some people may spend three to six months mainly building these capacities, and severe developmental trauma can take longer. I would treat that as a rough guide, not a countdown. If you are stable and have one clear target, you may be ready for focused trauma work much sooner.

Retiring or keeping a hypnosis shortcut

If you ever want to get rid of the shortcut, quit practicing it, take it out of your recordings and whatever apps you set it up in, and go back to your usual induction. Decide that while you’re wide awake, and say it to yourself. Don’t be surprised if it still works on you for a while, though. Saying it’s gone doesn’t erase it overnight. If it keeps kicking in when you don’t want it to, or it leaves you spacey and disoriented after you use it, get help from someone qualified. And if you’d rather keep it, that’s fine, as long as you only use it to start a session you’ve chosen for later. Either way, today’s trance ends when you come back.

A near-death comparison and its limit

The closest comparison that’s worth mentioning is the out-of-body perception in some near-death experiences. People sometimes report seeing things from impossible angles, and a controversial subset of those reports were verified by others in the room. Those cases had a target that other people could check, and my room experience didn’t, since I wasn’t floating around noticing specific details. So they can’t vouch for my event, but they help show what I mean by an experience that wasn’t stuck at the body’s vantage point anymore. If you want to dig in, there’s this systematic analysis of near-death reports, the AWARE-II cardiac-arrest study, and a recent veridical-NDE evidence scale.

Correcting a remembered quotation

I used to attribute “life is like a dream” directly to the historical Buddha. Turns out the famous stanza I loved comes from the later Mahāyāna Diamond Sutra, which puts some words in his mouth. The early Pāli texts do have images that are pretty close, though: SN 22.95 compares form to foam, feeling to a bubble, perception to a mirage and consciousness to a magic trick, and Dhammapada 170 asks us to see the world as a bubble and a mirage.
