## New durable lessons

### 1. Wanting vs needing is a useful human pressure, but do not flatten causes

A person can increasingly **need** a partner while being **wanted** less. That tension is useful only when the prose distinguishes why the person's own aliveness decreased.

- Blameless or largely involuntary diminishment: aging, illness, depression, grief, disability, etc. The hoped-for role of deep agape is that the partner may remain caring and perhaps erotically attached through some of this.
- Blameworthy self-abandonment/stagnation: becoming bored with life, letting the rest of one's world collapse, and turning the partner into the main supplier of warmth, meaning, reassurance, excitement, etc. In that case the author is entitled to assign more responsibility: one should not act shocked if the partner wants them less.

Do not put these in one morally flat list such as `age, illness, depression, stagnation, whatever` and then blame the whole list equally.

### 2. Eros is a drive, not merely a preference or judgment

Eros has propulsion. It pushes a person toward this particular partner: approach, vulnerability, sex, specialness, proximity, and risk before reciprocation is guaranteed.

This matters to the article's earlier reciprocity logic: somebody has to want enough to move first. Agape can discipline and support that drive; it does not generate the whole force of pursuit.

### 3. Eros can help build particular agape

Agape can exist toward anybody, but eros singles out one person and repeatedly pulls two people into enough intimacy and vulnerability for a much more particular agape to develop. That agape may eventually survive the drive that helped create it.

Do not turn this into a polished universal sequence in publication prose merely because the conceptual relation is clear.

### 4. `May stay` is not reassurance; the modal itself creates the crisis

The model repeatedly treated `he may stay even if eros is gone` as comforting. Joel identified the ordinary human reality the model missed: **may is two-sided**.

If a partner says, in effect, `Even if I stop wanting you, I may still stay because I love you`, the other person hears both:

- he may stay while no longer wanting me;
- he may leave anyway.

If she still erotically wants him, both possibilities can be frightening. His staying no longer proves that he wants her, while his agape also does not guarantee that he stays.

### 5. Agape's landing-pad role has a limit and a danger

Agape can rescue eros by keeping people from bolting during a temporary dip and giving erotic reciprocity a chance to recover.

But if eros is genuinely gone, agape can also keep a dead erotic relationship persisting. This can be especially painful when one partner still has eros. Do not portray survival of agape after eros as an automatically mature or reassuring endpoint.

### 6. Some couples may genuinely choose the bond after eros fades

Do not overcorrect in the other direction. Agape does not mechanically fail to support staying once eros is gone. Some mature couples may genuinely treasure the bond, shared life, care, and history more than the erotic drive that started it.

That is different from one partner staying mainly as a concession while the other still wants them. The latter may feel bad for both.

The key uncertainty is not whether staying can happen; it is whether anyone can **predict or promise in advance** which future they will inhabit.

### 7. Marriage-vow implication: a vow cannot guarantee the future emotional configuration

The article should not use `stay forever` as a solution to erotic insecurity. A vow can promise actions only insofar as the person believes they can deliver them; it cannot guarantee that future eros will remain, nor guarantee that future agape will feel sufficient to continue the same relationship form.

### 8. Timeline coherence matters more than transitional glue

A specific failure occurred when one paragraph jumped ahead to `what happens after eros fades`, followed by a paragraph explaining `how eros can weaken`. The second paragraph felt like it followed the first while actually rewinding the thought stream.

Repair by reordering the thought itself, not by inventing a transition.

### 9. Overcompletion remained the recurrent model temptation

Even after detector improvement, the model kept adding:

- explanatory sentences after the tension was already clear;
- tidy statements of what each love `decides`;
- neat resolution language that erased nuance;
- summaries after the stopping point.

The successful corrections generally made prior sentences carry more meaning rather than adding interpretive aftercare.

## Reality-check failure exposed

The model repeatedly reasoned from abstract relationship categories instead of checking what the proposed sentence would actually do to the partner hearing it. A good semantic sanity question for relational writing is:

> If one person actually said this sentence to the other person, what would the other person hear?

This caught the `may stay` failure immediately.
