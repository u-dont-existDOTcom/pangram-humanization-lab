# Romance Bundling Source Note — 2026-08-17

Purpose: decide whether bundling can serve as a compact historical example of a socially recognized intermediate stage between attraction and sexual/life entanglement.

## Scope

Minimal factual audit only. This is not a history-of-courtship section and should not become one.

## What the sources support

### Bundling was not an Amish invention and was geographically broader

Stephen R. Christ, `Bundling`, *Encyclopedia of Family Studies* (2016), DOI `10.1002/9781119085621.wbefs139`, describes bundling as a courtship practice found in conservative colonial North American communities and in Europe, involving a prospective couple sharing a bed while wrapped/clothed under norms against intercourse.

Richard Godbeer, `Courtship and Sexual Freedom in Eighteenth-Century America`, *OAH Magazine of History* 18(4), 2004, DOI `10.1093/maghis/18.4.9`, describes late-eighteenth-century New England courting couples spending nights together with parental permission; some parents required partial clothing/bundling as an attempt to prevent intercourse.

Leanne Calvert, `He came to her bed pretending courtship: sex, courtship and the making of marriage in Ulster, 1750–1844`, *Irish Historical Studies* 42(162), 2018, DOI `10.1017/ihs.2018.32`, documents bundling as recognized courtship behavior in Ulster as well, with couples sharing a bed fully clothed and engaging in non-penetrative intimacy.

Conclusion: wording such as `an old practice now especially associated with the Amish, though versions existed much more widely` is safer than calling bundling an Amish invention or uniquely Amish custom.

### Historical Amish practice is real, but the practice was already described as old/former rather than a normal current church custom

Calvin George Bachman, *The Old Order Amish of Lancaster County* (Pennsylvania German Society, Proceedings vol. 49, 1941/1942; later reprinted), devotes a chapter to bundling. Bachman says the evidence compels belief that bundling was formerly common among Amish people, cites an 1837 discipline condemning the youth practice, and quotes an Amish migration account describing a church established in Iowa in 1851 specifically to reject bundling.

Crucially, Bachman warns against representing bundling as a normal present-day Amish institution. He quotes a Pennsylvania-German authority saying that for over a hundred years no bundling had been going on among the Amish in that locality, acknowledges that some individuals might still engage in it, and states that the Amish church is definitely opposed to the practice.

Conclusion: `old, mostly extinct` is directionally defensible, but the article should not imply that contemporary Amish courtship generally includes bundling.

### Bundling's no-intercourse rule did not reliably prevent premarital pregnancy, but causation is not established

Godbeer reports that bundling/overnight courtship was intended in part to prevent intercourse, then notes that by the late eighteenth century roughly 30–40% of New England brides were already pregnant. He treats that high incidence as evidence that the containment strategy was not entirely effective.

This supports: `the rule clearly didn't always hold` or `high premarital pregnancy coexisted with the practice`.

It does **not** support: `bundling caused high pregnancy rates`, `bundling communities had higher pregnancy rates than otherwise comparable non-bundling communities`, or any single-cause claim.

## Article disposition

Use the example because it performs a concrete conceptual job and can stay compact. Avoid the supplied phrasing `In places where bundling was common, premarital pregnancy was common too` if it sounds like a comparative causal result. Prefer a historically anchored sentence about late-eighteenth-century New England.

Recommended article-level realization:

> Modern life barely has an intermediate stage between being interested in somebody and getting sexually entangled. One old example is bundling, a practice now especially associated with the Amish even though it existed much more widely. Courting couples could spend the night together in bed, usually clothed, with the idea that they could talk and be close without having sex. It sounds a lot like the missing middle I’m talking about. It also didn’t exactly solve the problem: in late-eighteenth-century New England, where bundling was common, 30–40 percent of brides were already pregnant. So I’m not proposing we bring bundling back. I’m saying we need some socially recognized way to explore tenderness and attraction without immediately sliding toward sex, cohabitation, and a life bond.

The percentage should be linked to Godbeer or expressed more generally if Joel prefers less historical detail.
