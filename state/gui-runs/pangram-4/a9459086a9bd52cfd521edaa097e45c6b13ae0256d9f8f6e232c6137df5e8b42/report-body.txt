### Also Look Outward
Don't make a whole diagnosis out of one ugly fight. They may come back later and actually talk about what you raised. They may still think you're wrong. You may still hate the boundary they're holding. That's annoying, but it isn't the same as watching hard conversations keep ending with your experience pushed aside. If that keeps happening, the pattern matters more than finding the perfect explanation for why they do it. [Lindsay Gibson](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VlNpgFWOLPw) writes about those repeated relational patterns in her work on emotional immaturity.
You may not know for certain whether the other person is or could be capable of a healthier response, and neither do they. If they promise to change, that's probably as useful as their prior promises were. Think about how long it has taken you to become more healthy. Could you have done that from one moment to the next based on a promise? Sometimes we can be a better support for someone when we maintain a realistic boundary, so that we can hold high expectations, and still feel prepared for their failure to meet them.
If you really did hurt them, repair it.
You can mean the apology. Then they're still upset, and you notice yourself starting to retract the complaint too. Maybe the first explanation was lousy and another one helps. After that, if you've said what you mean and they still don't understand, there may not be another sentence that fixes it. Guilt isn't a verdict on the whole argument, and it isn't proof they're manipulating you. Sometimes you say no and stop.
