# Before You Try to Go Deep
If you're so far inside the scared kid that even deciding what to do next feels impossible, don't turn “go deeper” into another job you have to perform. Get some support around you first. Come back when there's enough adult you available to make a choice and stick with it.
## You Don’t Need an Inner Monologue
If you're reading all this thinking, “But I don't have a voice in my head,” that's fine. When I say ask the child something, I don't mean you have to literally hear an answer. Maybe words come by themselves; maybe you have to make them on purpose. If the inside of your head stays quiet, ask out loud or write the question down. And if that still isn't how your mind works, maybe a drawing, a picture or song, a sensation or urge, or simply doing something caring is enough. You don't have to replace missing words with vivid pictures, either. Sometimes the mind is simply quiet. That doesn't mean you're resisting anything.
Sometimes the feeling lands first; other times the words do, and sometimes you can't tell. Leave it that way until words would actually help you figure out what happened or what to do. Thinking about it later isn't automatically avoidance, and feeling first doesn't settle what the feeling means.
## Regulation May Come Before Dialogue
And sometimes words aren't the problem at all; your body is just too charged.
If your solar plexus, abdomen, or somewhere else feels clenched and it's hard to relax into that heart-chakra love, try a slower, non-forced exhale or some gentle massage. I love using one foot to massage the other like a Yin/Yang symbol. Circular or spiral massage over the tense area can be nice too.
Sometimes you need more bottom-up help than that: Shaking, tapping (Emotional Freedom Technique), EMDR, Brainspotting, Somatic Experiencing, Water Therapy, Massage, Energy Healing, or another approach that works from the body upward.
The order matters here; sometimes you have to get the body down a notch before talking works.
If imagery starts carrying you away from the room you're actually sitting in, open your eyes. Look at the room, know what time it is, feel your body here. If a session leaves you more disoriented, more compelled, or functioning worse afterward, make it shorter next time—or stop and get grounded support.
### Catch the Hook Before You Interpret It
Before you know whether it's the child, a protector, or something old, you may already feel the hook. Maybe the jaw tightens, your belly contracts, heat comes up, or you feel yourself pull away. Sometimes you notice it only after you've already started doing something with it: halfway through a text, reaching for a drink, scrolling or overexplaining, attacking or disappearing, promising something, deciding what the whole relationship means. [Pema Chödrön](https://pemachodronfoundation.org/product/taking-the-leap-book/) uses the Tibetan word *shenpa* for that tightening and urge before the whole story has finished assembling itself.
Don't solve the relationship from there. Look around and admit, “I'm hooked.” Unless somebody needs protection right now, take one to three breaths before you say or do the thing you're dying to do. Feel the charge without making it prove anything yet. Maybe the adult move right now is warmth, protection, or direction; do one small thing from there. There may be something real happening with old stuff mixed into it, or you may simply have it wrong. You don't have to decide while you're at maximum activation.
For the next ten minutes, the meaning can wait. The text can sit unsent, the thing you're about to blurt out can wait, the drink can stay on the counter, the promise can wait, and so can the big decision.
Once you've come down a notch, ask what actually happened. Old material can get mixed into something that really happened now: somebody may have crossed a line and also woken up an old state or protector. You may find grief there, something to repair, or some care you never got. Keep the event separate from your verdict: “My stomach tightened” is one thing. “That person is evil” is another. What did they actually do? What supports that conclusion, what doesn't, and what else could fit? If literal words were in your head at the time, keep them as part of the event. Words you found later can still help you understand it, but they're the later translation.
If one breath is all you can do, do one breath. If turning inward starts making you less oriented, less functional, or more compelled, stop and get support from outside yourself.
### Also Look Outward
Inner-child work can make it too easy to explain everything as your wound. Sometimes there is a real problem in front of you. What happens when you disagree is more useful: can they look at what they did and make any room for your side, or does the whole conversation end with you carrying their feelings and dropping your own position? That matters more than a beautiful explanation for why they did it.
[Lindsay Gibson's work](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VlNpgFWOLPw) on emotional immaturity is a reminder to stop guessing motives for a minute and watch what the person keeps doing. Sometimes you've already explained enough.
People are allowed a bad day. They can disagree with you, take time, or set a boundary of their own. The question is what keeps happening. You may never know whether they can't respond differently, won't, or just couldn't that day. You can still stop counting on a response you aren't getting. If their behavior changes, change your assessment. Another promise by itself isn't that change, and you don't have to keep getting hurt just to collect more evidence.
Guilt doesn't settle it either. You may really have hurt somebody and need to repair it while also abandoning your own position because you need their disappointment to stop.
Before the next interaction, decide what enough would look like. Maybe mutual understanding isn't on offer. Getting one thing across, saying no, or ending the exchange before forty minutes of defending yourself can be enough. You don't have to make somebody understand before you act. While you're deciding, let the Witness notice what's happening inside and the Protector watch the actual interaction; the Guide can work with both.
### Keep the Draft. Edit the Response. Care for the Writer.
Write the message exactly as you want to send it. Then don't send it yet.
Come back and edit it without switching sides against yourself. Don't edit out a real protest, and don't dodge responsibility if it's yours; keep the request or boundary you actually meant. “Better” doesn't have to mean nicer. Maybe the final response is shorter; maybe you wait. If you need time, say so and actually come back when you said you would. If protection is needed now, don't wait for the exercise.
Then go back to the first draft and read it like it came from somebody you care about. What were they trying so hard to get across? What needed protecting or reassuring? That first draft isn't automatically childish; it may contain the sanest protest in the whole exchange. Edit the message without abandoning the person who wrote it.
### When More Processing Becomes the Hook
Healing can turn into checking too. The wound is real, and you're still asking for the hundredth time whether you finally grieved it correctly.
Handle the safety issue or practical thing that actually needs doing. After that, notice why you're doing another round. Did something new actually open? Did it change what you see or what you'll do? Or are you asking again because the last answer didn't feel certain enough? Grief can circle back without every return being a new request from the child.
If it's checking, let the thought stay unfinished and go back to the person, activity, or life you chose. You don't need to make it disappear first. Caring for the child doesn't require a meeting every time the mind says something.
Before another round, name what the exercise is supposed to change. You can leave a session calmer and full of insight while the original problem doesn't budge. If the practice gives you no useful contact—or mostly makes you monitor yourself more while daily life gets worse—stop answering that by doing more of it. Keep what actually helps and try another way of meeting the same need.
And if the exercise starts feeling like something you have to finish before you're allowed to get on with your life, stop there.
